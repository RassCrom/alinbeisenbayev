About 57 cities in Europe have a metro. Roughly 45 million people live within a 10-minute walk of a station, and more than 110 million live inside the boundaries of a city that has one. Most of them have stood in front of a metro map at some point. Have you ever wondered why the lines on it look so perfect, and why they so rarely follow the real shape of the city?

## A draughtsman's side project

It started in 1931, with a London Underground engineering draughtsman who thought he could change how people read metro maps. His name was Harry Beck.

At the time, metro maps were simply lines drawn over an ordinary city map, together with everything else on it: parks, rivers, streets. Beck thought this was too much clutter for someone who only wants to know where to change trains. So he challenged a convention that had stood for decades and drew something completely different:

- every line runs horizontally, vertically or at 45 degrees;
- stations are spaced evenly, wherever they really are;
- the crowded centre is enlarged and the long suburban branches are squeezed in.

He showed it to his bosses. It could have been a breakthrough. They rejected it as too radical.

## The second try

Beck didn't give up. He kept refining the design and tried again, and in 1933 the Underground agreed to a trial: 750,000 pocket copies. People loved it.

The idea at the core of Beck's map (simplify the geography, keep straight lines and consistent angles) has stayed with us ever since, on metro maps and on almost every other schematic map. Less, but better. Beck was 29 when he drew it.

On the schematic side, the network looks compact and evenly spread. In the real city, the lines bend around old streets, bunch together in the centre, and Line 12, MetroSur, turns out to be a loop of its own far south of the rest of the network.
